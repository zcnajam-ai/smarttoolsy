How to Calculate Percentages in Your Head: 6 Quick Tricks

Published 2026-10-24

You do not need a calculator for most everyday percentages. With a handful of shortcuts you can work out a tip, check a sale price or judge a pay rise in a few seconds. Here are six tricks that cover almost every situation.

1. Find 10% by moving the decimal point

To get 10% of any number, move the decimal point one place to the left. 10% of 250 is 25. 10% of 47.80 is 4.78. This is the foundation for nearly every other trick.

2. Build other percentages from 10%

  • 5% is half of 10%. 5% of 250 is 12.50.
  • 20% is double 10%. 20% of 250 is 50.
  • 15% is 10% plus 5%. 15% of 250 is 25 + 12.50 = 37.50.
  • 1% moves the decimal two places. 1% of 250 is 2.50, so 3% is 7.50.

Almost any percentage can be assembled this way. 18% is 20% minus 2%. 35% is 30% plus 5%.

3. Swap the numbers when it is easier

X% of Y always equals Y% of X. So 8% of 50 is the same as 50% of 8, which is obviously 4. Whenever one number is awkward and the other is friendly, flip them. 4% of 75 becomes 75% of 4, which is 3.

4. Work out a sale price in one step

Instead of finding the discount and subtracting it, find what you pay. 30% off means you pay 70%. On a 60 item, 70% of 60 is 42. That is one calculation instead of two.

For stacked discounts, multiply what you pay at each stage. 30% off then an extra 10% off means 70% of 90%, which is 63%, so the total discount is 37%, not 40%. Our discount calculator handles these combinations for you.

5. Percentage change: compare the difference to the starting point

To find how much something changed in percentage terms, divide the change by the original value. If rent goes from 800 to 880, the change is 80, and 80 is 10% of 800, so it rose 10%.

Always divide by the starting value. This is why a fall of 50% needs a rise of 100% to recover: from 100 to 50 is half, but from 50 back to 100 is double.

6. Estimate, then check

Round awkward numbers to friendly ones to get a quick estimate. 19% of 412 is close to 20% of 400, which is 80. The real answer is 78.28, so the estimate is good enough to judge whether a deal is worth it. When the exact figure matters, such as on an invoice, use the percentage calculator to confirm.

Practice examples

  1. A 15% tip on a bill of 64: 10% is 6.40, 5% is 3.20, total 9.60.
  2. 25% off a 120 jacket: you pay 75%, which is 90.
  3. A salary rising from 50,000 to 53,500: the change is 3,500, which is 7% of 50,000.

With a little practice these become automatic, and you will spot misleading “deals” much faster.
