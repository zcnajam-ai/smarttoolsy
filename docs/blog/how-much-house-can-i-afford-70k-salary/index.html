How Much House Can I Afford on a $70,000 Salary?

Published 2026-10-24

A $70,000 salary is close to the middle of the US income range, and it can buy a home in many parts of the country. How much house it buys depends less on the salary itself and more on four things: your other debts, your down payment, the interest rate you get, and local property taxes and insurance. This guide walks through the math so you can estimate your own number.

Start with the 28/36 rule

Many lenders and financial planners use the 28/36 rule as a starting point:

  • Spend no more than 28% of your gross (pre-tax) monthly income on housing: mortgage principal and interest, property tax, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance and HOA dues.
  • Keep all monthly debt payments, including housing, car loans, student loans and credit card minimums, at or below 36%.

On $70,000 a year, your gross monthly income is about $5,833. That gives:

  • A housing budget of about $1,633 a month (28%).
  • A total debt budget of about $2,100 a month (36%).

If your other debts are more than about $467 a month, the 36% limit becomes the tighter one. With a $700 car and student loan payment, for example, your housing budget drops to $2,100 − $700 = $1,400.

Turning $1,633 a month into a home price

The monthly payment is not just the loan. We used the mortgage calculator with these assumptions, which are typical but should be replaced with your local figures:

  • 30-year fixed-rate mortgage
  • Property tax of 1.1% of the price per year
  • Homeowners insurance of $1,800 per year
  • PMI of 0.6% of the loan per year when the down payment is under 20%
  • No HOA dues

Keeping the total payment at about $1,633 a month gives these maximum prices:

So on a $70k salary with no other large debts, a realistic price range is roughly $190,000 to $260,000. That is in the same neighborhood as the older rule of thumb that a home should cost about two and a half to three times your annual income ($175,000 to $210,000 here).

Why the down payment matters so much

Look across any row of the table: going from 10% to 20% down adds around $40,000 to your budget. Two things are happening. You borrow less, and you avoid private mortgage insurance (PMI), which lenders usually charge on conventional loans when you put down less than 20%. On a $189,000 loan, PMI at 0.6% adds about $95 a month.

A bigger down payment is not always the right move, though. Keep an emergency fund of several months of expenses after closing, and remember that closing costs typically add a few percent of the loan amount on top of the down payment.

Why the interest rate matters

Each half-point increase in the rate cuts your buying power by roughly $8,000 to $12,000 in these examples. Rates depend on the market, your credit score, your down payment and the loan type, so get quotes from at least three lenders. A better credit score before you apply can be worth more than months of extra saving.

Local costs can change the answer

Property tax rates differ enormously between states and even neighboring counties. Insurance costs are much higher in areas with hurricane, wildfire or flood risk, and a condo or planned community may charge HOA dues of a few hundred dollars a month. In the examples above, every extra $100 of monthly costs reduces the price you can afford by roughly $12,000 to $15,000. Look up the actual tax bill on listing pages and get an insurance quote before you make an offer.

A worked example

Suppose you find a $230,000 home, put 10% down and get a 6.5% rate. Using the same tax and insurance assumptions, the payment comes to about:

  • Principal and interest: $1,308
  • Property tax: $211
  • Insurance: $150
  • PMI: $104
  • Total: about $1,773 a month, or roughly 30% of gross income

That is slightly above the 28% guideline. A lender may still approve it, but you would have less room for savings and repairs. Options would be a larger down payment, a lower price, or shopping for a lower rate.

What lenders actually look at

The 28/36 rule is a guideline, not a law. Lenders look at your debt-to-income ratio, credit score, savings and job history, and some loan programs allow higher ratios. Being approved for a larger loan does not mean the payment will feel comfortable. Budget for maintenance too; many homeowners set aside around 1% of the home’s value each year for repairs.

Run your own numbers

Open the mortgage calculator, enter your expected rate, local tax rate and insurance, and adjust the home price until the monthly total matches your budget. Try the 15-year term as well to see how much interest you would save.
